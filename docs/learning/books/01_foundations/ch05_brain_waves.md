# Chapter 5: Reading Brain Waves

## The Secret Experiments

Jena, Germany, 1924. Dr. Hans Berger (21 May 1873 – 1 June 1941), a German psychiatrist, locks his laboratory door. He's working alone again, as he has for years. His colleagues don't know about these experiments. They'd mock him if they knew. They already think his ideas are crazy ([Haas, 2003, PubMed 16334737](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16334737/)).

On the table lies equipment he built himself: amplifiers, galvanometers, recording drums. Wires run to his patient - a young man who has volunteered for the mysterious experiment. Two electrodes are attached to the young man's scalp, one at the front, one at the back.

On July 6, 1924, at the University Hospital in Jena, Berger successfully recorded an electrical brain signal in humans for the first time. This first EEG recording was made on a 17-year-old boy during a neurosurgery performed by neurosurgeon Nikolai Guleke ([Stone & Hughes, 2013, Child's Nervous System](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00381-020-04564-z)).

But then he notices something. When the young man closes his eyes, the waves become larger and more regular. When he opens them, the waves shrink and speed up. This happens consistently, repeatedly.

Berger's heart races. He's seeing something real. Something coming from the brain itself. The waves correlate with the mental state of his subject!

After five years of secret work, Hans Berger has just recorded the first human brain waves.

---

## Why the Secrecy?

Why did Berger work in secret for so long? The answer reveals much about scientific culture and the courage needed for radical ideas.

Berger was a psychiatrist fascinated by the relationship between mind and brain. He believed that thoughts and consciousness had physical, measurable manifestations. In the early 1900s, this was controversial. Many scientists separated "mind" (psychology) from "brain" (biology).

Berger wanted to find objective measurements of mental states. Could he detect thinking electrically? Could he measure the difference between wakefulness and sleep? Between concentration and daydreaming?

His colleagues thought this was pseudoscience. The brain was too complex. Electrical signals would be too weak. The skull would block everything. Even if signals could be detected, they'd be random noise.

Previous attempts had failed. Researchers occasionally reported detecting brain electricity, but their results weren't reproducible. Many scientists concluded it was impossible.

Berger knew that announcing his experiments would invite ridicule. He might lose his position. His reputation would suffer. So he worked quietly, telling no one, until he had irrefutable evidence.

This took incredible patience and determination. For five years, he experimented alone. He built equipment. He recruited volunteers. He tried different electrode positions. He failed repeatedly. He persisted anyway.

> **Did You Know?**
>
> In 1893, when Berger was 19, he fell off his horse during military training and was nearly trampled. On that same day, his sister, far away, got a bad feeling about Hans and talked their father into sending a telegram asking if everything was all right. To young Berger, this eerie timing was no coincidence: It was a case of "spontaneous telepathy," he later wrote. He decided to study psychiatry to find a scientific basis for telepathy—though chasing telepathy was a dead end, in the attempt, Berger ended up inventing the EEG ([Science News, 2019](https://www.sciencenews.org/article/hans-berger-telepathy-neuroscience-brain-eeg)).

## Early Experiments: False Starts

Berger's first attempts began in 1920. He tried electrodes on exposed brain during surgeries. He got signals, but were they from the brain or from muscles in the scalp? Muscles generate strong electrical signals. Brain signals would be much weaker.

He tried recordings on patients with skull defects from injuries or surgery. With less bone in the way, signals should be clearer. But the results were inconsistent. Sometimes he saw rhythmic waves. Sometimes just noise.

He experimented with his own son, Klaus. Using small metal electrodes glued to the scalp, he made recordings for hours. He varied the conditions: eyes open, eyes closed, sleeping, doing math problems. He looked for patterns.

Gradually, patterns emerged. A particular rhythm appeared when subjects were relaxed with eyes closed. It happened consistently - about 10 cycles per second. When subjects opened their eyes or concentrated, this rhythm diminished or disappeared.

Berger called this the "alpha rhythm." It was the brain's signature electrical pattern during relaxed wakefulness.

He also noticed faster rhythms during mental activity and slower ones during drowsiness. Each mental state had a characteristic electrical pattern!

---

## The First Publication (1929)

In 1924, Berger succeeded in recording the first human electroencephalogram (EEG), a term he coined. However, filled with doubt, he took five years to publish his first paper in 1929, titled "Über das Elektrenkephalogramm des Menschen" ("On the Electroencephalogram of Man"). Always skeptical of his own research and afraid of unintended appropriation of his ideas, Berger worked in the evening between 5:00 and 8:00 p.m. in almost complete isolation ([Millett, 2001, PMC10756801](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10756801/)).

He described his methods, his observations, and his interpretations. He introduced the terms alpha and beta waves. Using the EEG, Berger was the first to describe the different waves or rhythms present in the normal and abnormal brain, such as the alpha wave rhythm (8-12 Hz), and its suppression (substitution by faster beta waves) when the subject opens the eyes—the so-called alpha blockade ([Stone & Hughes, 2013, Child's Nervous System](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00381-020-04564-z)).

The scientific community's response? Mostly silence. A few researchers were intrigued. Most ignored it. Some openly doubted his results.

Critics argued that his signals came from scalp muscles, not brain activity. They said his patterns were artifacts - electrical noise misinterpreted as meaningful signals. They questioned his equipment and methods.

Berger was devastated but not deterred. After 1929, Berger started to publish his reports in a series of 14 papers. By 1931, he had gathered 1133 records from 76 people, establishing normalization for alpha and beta waves in terms of frequency and amplitude.

But acceptance came slowly. Too slowly for Berger's fragile confidence.

---

## Breakthrough: Independent Confirmation

The turning point came in 1934. Adrian and Matthews, two highly respected British physiologists, decided to test Berger's claims. They built their own equipment and conducted their own experiments.

They succeeded. They confirmed everything Berger reported. The alpha rhythm was real. The changes with mental state were real. The signals truly came from the brain.

Adrian and Matthews published their confirmation in the prestigious journal Nature. They praised Berger's original discovery. They demonstrated the phenomenon to colleagues at conferences. The scientific community could no longer ignore it.

Berger's EEG was suddenly legitimate. Researchers worldwide began building EEG machines. Neurologists recognized its clinical potential. The field of electroencephalography exploded.

For Berger, the vindication came late. Years of isolation and doubt had damaged his mental health. He suffered from depression. Though he finally received recognition - including a Nobel Prize nomination - he never fully recovered emotionally.

Tragically, Hans Berger took his own life in 1941. But his discovery lived on, transforming neuroscience and medicine.

> **Did You Know?**
>
> Edgar Adrian, who confirmed Berger's findings, won the Nobel Prize in 1932 for his work on nerve function. He later said that Berger's EEG discovery deserved a Nobel Prize too, but Berger died before receiving one.

## Understanding Brain Waves

So what exactly are these brain waves? They're not like radio waves or sound waves. They're patterns of electrical activity from millions of neurons acting somewhat in synchrony.

Your brain contains roughly 86 billion neurons. Each one generates tiny electrical pulses constantly. When many neurons fire in sync, their combined electrical fields are strong enough to detect on the scalp.

Think of it like a stadium crowd. One person clapping is inaudible from outside. But thousands clapping in unison creates a sound you can hear blocks away. Brain waves are similar - individual neurons are too quiet, but synchronized groups create detectable signals.

Different mental states create different synchronization patterns:

**Alpha Waves (8-12 Hz)**: Present during relaxed wakefulness with eyes closed. They're most prominent in the back of the head. They represent a calm, idling brain - awake but not actively processing visual information.

**Beta Waves (12-30 Hz)**: Faster rhythms during active thinking, problem-solving, or focused attention. Your brain in beta is engaged and working. These waves dominate when your eyes are open and you're alert.

**Theta Waves (4-8 Hz)**: Slower rhythms during drowsiness, light sleep, and deep meditation. Children show more theta than adults. These waves are associated with memory processing and creativity.

**Delta Waves (0.5-4 Hz)**: The slowest waves, prominent during deep sleep. Your brain in delta is in its most restorative state. These high-amplitude, slow waves characterize unconscious sleep stages.

Different brain regions also generate different patterns. Visual areas respond to visual stimulation. Motor areas activate during movement planning. Language areas show activity during speech.

The EEG reveals all this by showing the brain's electrical "voice."

## Medical Applications Emerge

Once accepted, the EEG quickly proved medically valuable. Neurologists discovered that different brain disorders produced distinctive EEG patterns.

**Epilepsy**: Perhaps the most important application. Epilepsy involves abnormal, synchronized neural firing - electrical storms in the brain. The EEG captures these dramatically. Neurologists could diagnose epilepsy, determine seizure types, locate seizure origins, and assess treatment effectiveness.

**Sleep Disorders**: The EEG revealed that sleep has stages, each with characteristic brain wave patterns. This enabled systematic sleep studies. Disorders like sleep apnea, narcolepsy, and insomnia could be objectively assessed.

**Brain Tumors**: Tumors disrupted normal brain electrical activity. EEG could detect abnormal patterns suggesting tumor presence, guiding further investigation.

**Brain Death**: The EEG became a standard tool for confirming brain death. A flat line - no electrical activity - indicated that brain function had ceased permanently.

**Cognitive Function**: Researchers used EEG to study attention, memory, language, and perception. They could watch brain activity change in real-time as people performed mental tasks.

The EEG provided a window into brain function that didn't exist before. It was non-invasive, painless, and safe. It could be repeated as often as needed.

## How Modern EEG Works

Today's EEG is far more sophisticated than Berger's original setup, but the principle remains identical. Multiple electrodes (usually 19-256) are attached to the scalp using a special cap or paste. Each electrode detects voltage changes from nearby brain activity.

The signals are amplified millions of times - brain voltages are only microvolts. They're filtered to remove noise from muscles, heart, and power lines. They're digitized for computer storage and analysis.

Modern systems show multiple channels simultaneously - one for each electrode pair. This creates a rich picture of brain activity across the entire head. Specialists learn to recognize hundreds of patterns.

Computer algorithms now assist interpretation. They can automatically detect epileptic spikes, sleep stages, and abnormal patterns. But human expertise remains essential - the brain is too complex for full automation.

Portable EEG devices now exist. Some are wireless, using Bluetooth to transmit data. Research-grade EEG caps are used in brain-computer interfaces, allowing paralyzed patients to control devices with thoughts.

Consumer devices like meditation headbands and sleep monitors use simplified EEG technology. While less precise than medical-grade equipment, they demonstrate how accessible brain monitoring has become.

> **Did You Know?**
>
> Some modern EEG systems can record from 256 electrodes simultaneously! This high-density EEG provides incredibly detailed maps of brain activity, showing which brain regions activate during specific tasks.

## Beyond Medicine: Research Applications

The EEG became a powerful research tool for understanding how the brain works. Cognitive neuroscientists use it to study:

**Attention**: How brain activity differs between focused attention and mind-wandering. What happens when attention shifts. How the brain filters relevant from irrelevant information.

**Memory**: Brain patterns during memory formation versus recall. How sleep consolidates memories. What happens when memories are forgotten.

**Language**: Brain responses to words, grammar, and meaning. How bilingual brains switch languages. Processing differences between written and spoken language.

**Emotion**: Electrical patterns associated with different emotional states. How emotion and cognition interact. Brain markers of depression and anxiety.

**Development**: How brain wave patterns change from infancy through old age. What normal development looks like. When deviations suggest problems.

The EEG's advantages for research include:
- **Temporal precision**: It shows changes millisecond by millisecond
- **Cost**: Much cheaper than fMRI or PET scans
- **Convenience**: Participants can move somewhat naturally
- **Safety**: No radiation, no contraindications, repeatable endlessly

Thousands of EEG research papers are published annually. Each adds to our understanding of how the mind emerges from brain activity.

## The Consciousness Question

Berger's original obsession was consciousness itself. Could the EEG reveal the physical basis of awareness, thought, and subjective experience? This remains one of neuroscience's deepest questions.

The EEG shows clear differences between conscious and unconscious states. Wakefulness has one pattern. Deep sleep has another. Anesthesia creates yet another. Brain death shows no pattern at all.

But does the EEG explain consciousness? Can electrical waves account for subjective experience - the feeling of seeing red, tasting chocolate, or feeling happy? Most scientists say no - the EEG is necessary but not sufficient to explain consciousness.

However, the EEG proves that consciousness has physical correlates. Mental states aren't separate from brain states - they are brain states. Changing brain electricity changes mental experience. This was Berger's fundamental insight.

Modern consciousness research builds on his foundation. Using EEG combined with other techniques, researchers probe the neural basis of awareness. They study what makes some brain activity conscious while other activity remains unconscious.

The mystery isn't solved, but the EEG provided crucial evidence that minds and brains aren't separate - they're two ways of describing the same thing.

## What's Next?

By the mid-20th century, medicine had tools for measuring the body's main electrical signals. The ECG revealed heart activity. The EEG showed brain waves. EMG tracked muscle signals. These tools revolutionized diagnosis and research.

But all these techniques had limitations. The recordings were analog - ink on paper. Storage was difficult. Analysis was manual and slow. Sharing data required physically mailing paper charts.

Then computers arrived. Everything was about to change. Digital technology would transform how biosignals were recorded, stored, analyzed, and shared. The data revolution was beginning.

Chapter 6 explores how computers entered medicine. You'll learn how analog signals become digital data. How sampling works. Why the digital revolution made new types of analysis possible. And how it created challenges we're still solving today.

The age of computational medicine was dawning.

**Chapter 5 Summary**: Hans Berger worked in secret for years before publishing the first human EEG in 1929. He discovered alpha rhythms (8-12 Hz) during relaxed wakefulness and showed that brain waves changed with mental states. Initial skepticism ended when Adrian and Matthews confirmed his findings in 1934. The EEG revealed that different mental states produce different electrical patterns: alpha, beta, theta, and delta waves. It became essential for diagnosing epilepsy, sleep disorders, and brain death. Modern EEG uses dozens to hundreds of electrodes and computer analysis. Berger's discovery proved that consciousness has physical, measurable correlates and established electroencephalography as a cornerstone of neuroscience.
